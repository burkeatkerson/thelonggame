**What is the 70% rule?** A screening formula for flips: pay no more than 70%
of the after-repair value (ARV) minus repair costs. For a house worth
$300,000 fixed up and needing $40,000 of work, the maximum offer is $300,000
× 0.70 − $40,000 = **$170,000**. The 30% you're *not* paying isn't profit —
it's selling costs, holding costs, financing costs, and your margin, in that
order. Understand what lives inside the 30% and you'll also understand when
the rule should be 75% — or 65%.

That ~15% margin is the payment for risk, work, and the months of your life —
and it's what evaporates first when the rehab runs over or the market cools
mid-project. The rule's discount exists because flips have brutal fixed
costs: you pay full transaction costs on both ends of a short hold.

<Callout>
  The 70% rule is a unit of discipline, not a law of nature. The number that
  matters is the dollars of margin left after honest costs — the percentage
  is just how you find it fast.
</Callout>

The discipline has a body count behind it: industry data suggests close to
half of first flips lose money or break even, and virtually every one of those
losses walked in through a massaged MAO — ARV nudged up, repairs nudged down,
until the deal someone wanted passed the screen someone else invented to stop
it. The formula only protects people who feed it honest inputs. That's not a
caveat; it's the entire instruction.

## Flipping's place in the long game

A flip converts skill and nerve into a taxed lump of capital — ordinary
income rates, no depreciation shelter, no
[step-up ending](/articles/1031-chains-and-the-step-up-in-basis). That makes
flipping a fine *engine* and a poor *destination*: the
[twenty-year math](/articles/the-twenty-year-math-nobody-shows-you) favors
holding. Flip to build the capital pile; then let the
[BRRRR method](/articles/brrrr-method-complete-guide) and the Cashflow pillar
put that pile to work in assets you never have to sell.
