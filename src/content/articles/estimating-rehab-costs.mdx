**How do you estimate renovation costs?** At three levels of precision, each matched to a decision: **napkin bands** ($/sqft by renovation tier — enough to screen a deal in sixty seconds), the **walk-through method** (room-by-room and system-by-system pricing during a showing — enough to make an offer), and the **line-item scope of work** (quantity-times-price job costing — the document your [contractor bids against](/articles/hiring-and-managing-contractors) and your [lender draws against](/articles/fha-203k-and-renovation-loans)). Beginners lose money by using level one where level three was owed; professionals move through all three on every deal, each level tightening the last. The estimate is half of your offer math — [ARV is the other half](/articles/how-to-determine-arv) — and unlike ARV, this number you can actually control.

## Level 2: the walk-through method

Offer-grade estimating prices the house as **five systems plus rooms**, because that's how renovation dollars actually cluster. The big five first — they're binary and large: **roof** ($8–15k asphalt replacement; read the ceiling stains), **HVAC** ($6–12k full replacement; check the data plate's age), **electrical** ($8–15k for a rewire/panel; knob-and-tube or a fuse box decides it), **plumbing** ($5–15k repipe; galvanized supply lines decide it), **foundation** (the walk-away conversation — get a structural opinion before contracting anything with stair-step cracks). Then the money rooms: **kitchen** ($12–25k mid-grade full redo), **baths** ($6–12k each full), and **per-room cosmetics** ($2.5–4k a room covers paint, flooring, doors, hardware, lighting). Add exterior (siding, windows at $400–700 each installed, landscaping), then the forgotten ten (below), then contingency. A disciplined investor walks a house with this checklist in 30 minutes and lands within 15% of final cost — which, [run through MAO math](/articles/70-percent-rule-house-flipping), is offer-grade.

## Level 3: the scope of work — real job costing

Under contract, the estimate becomes a **scope of work**: every line item with location, specification, quantity, material allowance, and labor — "Kitchen: 14 LF shaker cabinets (allowance $210/LF installed); 32 sqft quartz ($68/sqft installed); GE slate appliance package ($3,400)…" — organized by trade and sequenced by construction order (demo → structural → rough mechanicals → insulation/drywall → finishes → punch). This document is the entire management system in embryo: contractors **bid against it** (three bids on the *same* scope is the only real price discovery — vague scopes produce unbiddable bids and infinite change orders), the **draw schedule** pays against its completed sections, **allowances** cap the selections that blow budgets (every "we'll pick the tile later" is an unpriced liability), and variance tracking against it is how you learn — your actual-vs-estimate file across five projects is the most valuable proprietary data a renovator owns. Software (FlipperForce-class tools, or a rigorous spreadsheet) helps; the discipline is the asset. Two closing rules from the [job-site graduates](/articles/spec-home-building): **walk the house with your contractor before finalizing any budget you'll be held to** — their eyes on-site beat your checklist — and **never let the contingency migrate into the scope**; it's insurance against the wall you haven't opened, and pre-1950 houses always have one.

<Callout>
The renovation budget isn't a guess that gets refined — it's three different documents for three different decisions. Screen with bands, offer with the walk-through, and sign nothing against anything less than a line-item scope.
</Callout>
