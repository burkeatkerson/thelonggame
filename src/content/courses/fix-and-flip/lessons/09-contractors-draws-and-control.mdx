Contractor failure ruins more first flips than bad markets ever have — and nearly all of it is preventable with structure you now already own: the Lesson 9 scope. This lesson turns it into a hiring process, a payment architecture, and a weekly rhythm. The goal isn't suspicion; it's a system so clean that **everyone can be trusted because nobody has to be.**

## Hiring: the referral economy plus one test

Good contractors are booked, not Googling — find them where they already work: other investors' crews (your [REIA and buyer-list network](/articles/wholesaling-real-estate-complete-guide)), the supply-house counter ("who buys in volume and pays their account?"), inspectors and agents who see workmanship *after* the fact, and clean job sites you stop at. Verify like an underwriter — license and insurance called in, references old and new — then run the professional's move: **a small paid test project** ($3–5k bath or a punch-list week) before anyone touches your full renovation. → [The complete hiring system](/articles/hiring-and-managing-contractors)

**Bid it three ways, one scope.** Identical scope to three contractors makes bids finally comparable. Toss the outlier 25% below the cluster — that's not a bargain, it's a change-order business plan — and interrogate line-level gaps: they're showing you where your scope was vague.

## The payment architecture

One principle runs everything: **completed work releases money; money never advances ahead of work.**

## The weekly rhythm

**One scheduled walk** (same day weekly, against the scope, photographing everything — especially inside walls before they close: that photo file settles disputes, feeds your variance data, and documents your build for the appraiser and buyer's inspector later). **One channel** for all decisions and selections — texts scatter; a single thread holds the record. **Selections delivered ahead of need** — your slow tile decision is his idle crew, and the owner is every schedule's most common bottleneck. And **pay earned draws same-day** — contractors triage clients by payment reliability, and the investor who inspects promptly and pays instantly gets the callback, the honest number, and the crew in January. That reputation compounds across every project you'll ever run; it's the [repeatable-crew asset](/articles/spec-home-building) this whole business is secretly building.

**The pitfall to skip:** managing by relationship instead of by document because the contractor is likable. The likable ones deserve the clean system too — it's what lets you hire them again with the friendship intact.

**Do this now:** Build your one-page contract checklist from this lesson (scope attached · fixed price · draw schedule · written changes only · timeline with daily late credit · lien waivers · insurance verified) and have a local attorney review your template once. It'll cost a few hundred dollars and govern a decade of projects.
