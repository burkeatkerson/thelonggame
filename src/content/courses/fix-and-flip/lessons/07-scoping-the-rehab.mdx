You screened with napkin bands in Lesson 4. Now the skill that turns a showing into an offer: pricing a renovation **room by room and system by system in thirty minutes**, accurately enough to sign a contract against. This is the walk-through method — and it starts with the five checks that decide whether you should be estimating at all.

## First: the big five (and the walk-away signals)

Renovation dollars cluster in systems, so systems get checked first — each is large, binary, and readable in minutes:

| System | The check | Replace cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Roof** | Ceiling stains inside, shingle condition, sag | $8–15k |
| **HVAC** | Data plate age (15+ years = budget it), does it run | $6–12k |
| **Electrical** | Panel type — fuse box or knob-and-tube = rewire | $8–15k |
| **Plumbing** | Galvanized supply, water pressure, water heater age | $5–15k |
| **Foundation** | Stair-step cracks, sloping floors, doors that don't close | **Get a structural opinion first** |

**Walk-away signals** — not "price it higher," walk (or reprice as a different deal entirely): active foundation movement without an engineer's number, widespread mold from an unresolved water source, fire damage beyond one room, and pre-1976 mobiles or unpermitted additions your buyer's lender will reject. The most profitable sentence in flipping is *"this one's not for me"* said in minute six.

## Then: money rooms and the per-room rate

**Kitchens ($12–25k full mid-grade)** and **baths ($6–12k each)** get priced individually — they're 40% of most budgets and 90% of the buyer's decision. Everything else rides a per-room rate: **$2.5–4k per room** covers paint, LVP, doors, hardware, and lighting at rental-to-retail grade. Add exterior line items (windows $400–700 installed each, siding, landscaping curb-appeal minimum $2–4k), then the **forgotten ten** — permits, dumpsters (plural), utilities during rehab, builder's risk insurance, re-inspections, punch list, staging — a routine $5–10k the napkin never saw. Then **contingency as a real line: 10% cosmetic, 15% moderate, 20%+ on guts and anything pre-1950.** It will be spent. ## Design to the exit, not to yourself

The scope's strategic rule: **renovate to the neighborhood's comps, not your taste.** Your [Lesson 6 comps](/articles/how-to-determine-arv) literally show you the finish level the ARV pays for — matching it is the job; exceeding it is charity to the next owner. The buyer-psychology priorities, in order: kitchen, primary bath, curb appeal, light (paint + fixtures), flooring continuity. The classic over-improvements — the $8k appliance package in a $240k neighborhood, the bathroom addition the comps don't reward — are how decent deals become break-evens with beautiful photos.

<Callout>
The walk-through prices two systems and two rooms carefully and lets everything else ride a rate — because two systems and two rooms are 70% of every budget. Precision goes where the dollars are.
</Callout>

**The pitfall to skip:** estimating from the listing photos. Photos hide the panel box, the water heater's date, and the smell — every five-figure surprise lives in exactly the places photos don't go.

**Do this now:** Print the checklist (big five → kitchen/baths → per-room → exterior → forgotten ten → contingency) and walk any three houses with it this month — open houses count. Time yourself. By the third walk-through you'll be under 40 minutes and within striking distance of a contractor's number, which is exactly where Lesson 9 picks up.
